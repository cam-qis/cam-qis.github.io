About me

I am interested in using solid-state atomic, molecular, and optical (AMO) quantum systems to build new technologies and investigate fundamental physics. My research follows two related directions: scaling quantum information systems and probing fundamental physics with precision quantum measurements.

Research

My research is motivated by what becomes possible when we can prepare, control, and measure quantum states in a solid. The same tools that allow us to build quantum technologies can also turn a crystal into a remarkably sensitive instrument for studying fundamental physics. I am interested in both directions.

Scaling quantum information systems

How do we turn a well-controlled quantum system into a useful, large-scale machine? Building better qubits is only part of the challenge. We also need ways to connect them, control them, and move information between them without losing the quantum properties we are trying to preserve.

Solid-state color centers are particularly interesting because they combine atom-like optical and spin states with the possibility of fabrication and integration. My work has involved photonic circuits for silicon-vacancy (SiV) centers in diamond and the optical characterization and integration of T centers in silicon. These systems raise questions about how light can serve as an interface to stationary quantum states, and how device physics and coherent control shape what can ultimately be built.

At a larger scale, I am interested in the architecture of fault-tolerant quantum computers. In modular systems, arranging logical qubits, scheduling lattice-surgery operations, and routing quantum information can be as consequential as improving an individual physical device. I have explored these tradeoffs through mapping, scheduling, and resource analysis of quantum circuits. The connection between these scales is what excites me: the physics of a single quantum interface constrains the whole machine, while system-level requirements tell us which devices are worth engineering.

Probing fundamental physics

Why is the universe made of matter rather than equal amounts of matter and antimatter? Part of the answer may lie in subtle asymmetries in nature's laws that have yet to be discovered. Searches for new sources of time-reversal (T) symmetry violation offer one way to look for such physics. Rather than producing new particles in a collider, the idea is to measure familiar quantum systems precisely enough to reveal tiny interactions that would otherwise be invisible.

I am interested in using europium-doped yttrium orthosilicate (Eu:YSO) for this purpose. Europium nuclear spins in the crystal can retain coherence for exceptionally long times, while their local electric environment provides sensitivity to interactions associated with a possible nuclear Schiff moment. A symmetry-violating interaction could appear as an extremely small shift in a spin-resonance frequency. By comparing ions with opposite local crystal polarizations and carefully rejecting ordinary magnetic-field effects, we can search for a signal far smaller than the backgrounds surrounding it.

What draws me to this experiment is the contrast in scale: using coherent control of spins inside a crystal to look for new symmetry-violating physics, and perhaps gain clues about one of the largest unanswered questions in cosmology. Such a measurement would not by itself explain the universe's matter–antimatter imbalance, but it could help test the physics that might underlie it.
