About me

I am interested in using solid-state atomic, molecular, and optical (AMO) quantum systems to build new technologies and investigate fundamental physics. My research follows two related directions: scaling quantum information systems and probing fundamental physics with precision quantum measurements.

Research

Optically addressable spins in solids bridge atomic physics and engineered quantum devices. Their coherence and controllable light–matter interactions make them attractive for quantum information processing, while their sensitivity to weak perturbations provides a platform for precision tests of fundamental physics.

Scaling quantum information systems

Large-scale quantum information processing requires the ability to preserve, manipulate, and distribute quantum states across many physical components. Optically active point defects, including silicon-vacancy (SiV) centers in diamond and T centers in silicon, combine localized spin states with optical transitions that can mediate spin–photon interfaces. Integrating these centers with nanophotonic structures offers a route toward efficient connections between stationary quantum memories and traveling photons, with applications in quantum networking and distributed quantum computing.

Scaling beyond individual devices also introduces architectural challenges. In modular fault-tolerant quantum computers, the placement of logical qubits, scheduling of lattice-surgery operations, and movement of quantum information between modules determine communication overhead, execution time, and hardware requirements. Mapping, scheduling, and resource analysis link the constraints of physical devices to those of large quantum algorithms. Understanding these connections is essential for designing quantum processors that can scale beyond isolated demonstrations.

Probing fundamental physics

The origin of the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe remains an open question in fundamental physics. Known sources of charge–parity (CP) violation in the Standard Model appear insufficient to explain the observed imbalance, motivating searches for additional symmetry-violating interactions. Precision measurements of electric dipole moments and related nuclear Schiff moments offer sensitive probes of parity (P)- and time-reversal (T)-violating physics, complementary to searches at particle accelerators.

Rare-earth-doped crystals provide a promising platform for such measurements. Europium ions in yttrium orthosilicate (Eu:YSO) exhibit long-lived nuclear-spin coherence and occupy locally noncentrosymmetric lattice sites, where a nonzero nuclear Schiff moment could produce a minute shift in spin-resonance frequencies. Spectroscopy of symmetry-related ion ensembles and intrinsic comagnetometry can help distinguish a T-violating signal from ordinary magnetic-field shifts. Combining coherent control, optical readout, and differential measurements in a crystal offers a route to testing fundamental symmetries with solid-state quantum systems.
