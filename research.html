Research

My research explores solid-state AMO quantum technologies for two complementary purposes: scaling quantum information systems and answering questions in fundamental physics. Both directions rely on the ability to understand, control, and measure quantum states with high precision, while linking microscopic physics to practical experimental systems.

1. Scaling quantum information systems

How do we move from isolated, well-controlled quantum devices to useful, large-scale quantum systems? I am interested in this question from the device level through the system architecture: developing solid-state spin–photon interfaces, integrating quantum emitters with photonic hardware, and understanding how physical constraints shape fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Silicon-vacancy centers in diamond — MITRE

Work involving SiV color centers, a promising solid-state platform for optically interfaced quantum memories and networked quantum systems.

T centers in silicon — UC Berkeley

Experimental quantum-device and photonics work involving T centers in silicon, with an emphasis on optical characterization, device integration, and the physical interfaces needed for quantum information processing.

Modular quantum computing architectures — EPFL

Mapping, scheduling, and resource analysis for fault-tolerant modular architectures, including lattice-surgery operations and the communication demands of distributed logical qubits. This connects qubit-level capabilities to the design of scalable quantum computing systems.

2. Probing fundamental physics with quantum systems

Precisely controlled quantum systems can also act as sensors for physical effects far beyond the reach of ordinary measurements. I am interested in using the atomic-like states of solid-state systems to test fundamental symmetries and search for signatures of physics beyond the Standard Model.

Precision measurement with Eu:YSO

My current research uses europium-doped yttrium orthosilicate (Eu:YSO) as a platform for precision spectroscopy and coherent spin control. The aim is to probe extremely small symmetry-violating energy shifts, including potential signatures associated with time-reversal violation and nuclear Schiff moments. This effort brings together AMO physics, solid-state quantum control, and the engineering of sensitive measurement systems.
